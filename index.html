How it works

Each player's and team's average before GTA VI came out, next to their average since. Whoever fell off the most is at the top.

TL;DR

  1. Everyone is measured against their own numbers, not against other players. "Before" is the season up to the night of November 18. "After" is every game from November 19 on.
  2. Each side's average comes from adding up the totals and dividing once, like total points over total games. That's how box-score averages are normally done, and it means a five-minute cameo doesn't count the same as a 40-minute night.
  3. The slump is how far the average fell, as a percentage of where it started. Going from 20 points a game to 15 is a 25% drop.
  4. Stars are handed out relative to whoever you're looking at. Everyone who dropped gets ranked and split into six groups. The worst sixth gets six stars, the mildest sixth gets one. So if the biggest drop in the league is only 5%, that player still gets six stars.
  5. Early on, one bad game can land someone at the top of the list. That's on purpose. Use Week 1 or Weeks 1–2 if you want to look at a set window.

The math

slump % = before average − after averagebefore average

One adjustment: if a player's "before" number is tiny, the percentage gets silly fast. A bench guy going from 1 point a game to 0 would be "down 100%". So each sport has a floor for the number the drop is divided by. The floors are listed below.

Stars

Basketball

NBA

Players are scored on Game Score

Game Score is a stat John Hollinger came up with to sum up a player's night in one number. It adds up the good stuff (points, rebounds, assists, steals, blocks) and takes away for missed shots, fouls and turnovers. A solid starter is usually around 10; a big night is 25 or more.

PTS + 0.4·FGM − 0.7·FGA − 0.4·(FTA − FTM) + 0.7·OREB + 0.3·DREB + STL + 0.7·AST + 0.7·BLK − 0.4·PF − TOV

By default it's shown per 36 minutes, which is total Game Score divided by total minutes, times 36. That way a player whose minutes got cut doesn't look like he's playing worse. If you'd rather see straight per-game numbers, switch to Per game in Adjust metrics.

Hover over a player's Game Score change to see how much of his drop came from playing fewer minutes and how much came from doing less with the minutes he had.

Hockey

NHL

Skaters are scored on Impact

Impact = goals + 0.7 × assists + 0.1 × shots on goal, per game

Goals don't come often, and a good player can go a week without one. Counting shots a little gives credit to the guy who keeps firing even when nothing goes in. Assists count for a bit less than goals.

Turn on shooting in Adjust metrics to see shooting percentage (goals divided by shots) and points per game.

Football

NFL

Players are scored on EPA per play

EPA stands for expected points added. Before every snap, an offense has a certain number of points it can expect to score on that drive, based on the down, the distance and where it is on the field. A 15-yard catch on 3rd and 10 raises that number a lot. A sack lowers it. EPA is the change, credited to the player who threw, ran or caught the ball.

Dividing by plays gives a rate that works for any position. Above zero means a player's plays help more than an average play would; a strong quarterback usually sits around +0.15. It's the stat NFL analysts lean on most for exactly this reason: it accounts for situation, which yards and touchdowns don't.

EPA per play = (passing EPA + rushing EPA + receiving EPA) ÷ (pass attempts + sacks + carries + targets)

The tables also show EPA per game, plays per game and total yards per game.

Teams are scored on win percentage

Wins divided by games played, with a tie counting as half a win. NFL teams only play once a week, so for the first while each team's "after" record is just a game or two. Expect some big swings.

Players covered
Anyone with enough work before launch: 100 plays for quarterbacks, 50 for running backs, 30 targets for receivers and tight ends

Floors

Player numbers come from nflverse, the open NFL data project most public analysts use, which publishes every player's weekly stats with EPA. Defensive players and kickers aren't rated.

The fine print

  • Nobody can actually tell whether a player is playing GTA. Injuries, trades, a brutal road trip or a hot start cooling off can all look the same in the numbers.
  • The first few days will be noisy. After one or two games almost anyone can look like they've fallen apart. It evens out as more games come in.
  • Because stars are relative, they change with what you're looking at. A player might have four stars when you're viewing everyone and five when you filter to just the NBA.
  • The "before" numbers stop updating at launch. They're this season through November 18, not last season.
  • Scores, standings and box scores come from Sportradar. Season player stats come from Basketball-Reference, Hockey-Reference and nflverse. Everything updates each morning around 5am Eastern.
